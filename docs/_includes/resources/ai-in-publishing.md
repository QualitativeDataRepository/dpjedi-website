## General resources about AI in publishing  {#general-resources}

[COPE has a landing page (“COPE Focus”) dedicated to the topic of artificial intelligence](https://publicationethics.org/cope-focus/artificial-intelligence){:target="_blank"} which gathers links to discussions, cases, recorded talks, and other guidance documents related to the intersection of AI and publishing (note that some resources require a free guest account to access).

A JAMA editorial provides "[Guidance for Authors, Peer Reviewers, and Editors on Use of AI, Language Models, and Chatbots](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/fullarticle/2807956){:target="_blank"}", outlining its new policies regarding the topic, and citing preliminary evidence on the promises and limitations of this technology for peer review. 

The International Science Council (ISC) is working with COPE, STM, World Conferences on Research Integrity and the Global Young Academy to create a [Global Reporting Standard for AI Disclosure in Research](https://council.science/our-work/ai-disclosure-in-research/){:target="_blank"}. 

Cambridge University Press conducted a community-led review of the open research publishing ecosystem, resulting in [a report (“Publishing Futures”) focused on identifying innovative solutions to complex current issues](https://www.cambridge.org/br/universitypress/what-we-offer/research-publishing/publishing-futures){:target="_blank"}.

## AI use by authors (in research and/or manuscript writing) {#use-by-authors}

Some general consensus appears to be emerging on how artificial intelligence tools may or may not be incorporated into the scientific process, and several entities (e.g., [COPE](https://publicationethics.org/guidance/cope-position/authorship-and-ai-tools){:target="_blank"}, [JAMA](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/fullarticle/2807956){:target="_blank"}, [Cambridge](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/publishing-ethics/authorship-and-contributorship-journals#ai-contributions-to-research-content){:target="_blank"}, [Oxford](https://academic.oup.com/pages/for-authors/books/author-use-of-artificial-intelligence){:target="_blank"}) support the following broad guidelines:

* **AI tools, AI agents, LLMs, and chatbots cannot be considered or listed as an author of a paper**.  
  COPE: “AI tools cannot meet the requirements for authorship as they cannot take responsibility for the submitted work. As non-legal entities, they cannot assert the presence or absence of conflicts of interest nor manage copyright and license agreements.”  
  Oxford: “Gen AI does not qualify as an author and should not be used to undertake primary authorial responsibilities, such as generating arguments and scientific insights, writing analysis, or drawing conclusions.  
    
* **Any use of AI tools must be transparently disclosed within the manuscript.**   
  COPE: “Authors who use AI tools in the writing of a manuscript, production of images or graphical elements of the paper, or in the collection and analysis of data, must be transparent in disclosing”  
  Cambridge: “AI use must be declared and clearly explained in publications such as research papers, just as we expect scholars to do with other software, tools and methodologies.”  
  Oxford: “The use, or proposed use, of any Gen AI tool in content generation or preparation must be disclosed in detail to your Acquisitions Editor at the earliest stage of discussion, whether proposal submission, research, or manuscript writing.

* **Authors are fully and solely responsible for the content of their manuscript**, whether produced by humans or AI tools.   
  JAMA**:** “authors must take responsibility for the integrity of the content generated by these models and tools.”  
  Cambridge: “Authors are accountable for the accuracy, integrity and originality of their research papers, including for any use of AI.”  
  Oxford: “Authors remain fully accountable for the accuracy, integrity, and originality of their works, including any Gen AI-created content these works may contain.

### Tools for AI Use Disclosure {#tools-for-disclosure}

Many journal policies allow the use of AI tools in the scientific process in various ways, provided that this use is transparently disclosed by the authors. There is currently no consensus on how this disclosure should happen, but there are some tools and guidelines to facilitate accurate disclosure:

* [An Initial Scholarly AI Taxonomy](https://upstream.force11.org/an-initial-scholarly-ai-taxonomy/){:target="_blank"}, which describes multiple ways in which AI tools may be used within the scientific process (e.g., to “Analyse \- Examine patterns, relationships, or trends within the information”), and includes examples for each type. It may help authors more accurately describe the use of AI tools in the scientific process.  
* [Recommendations for a Classification of AI Use in Academic Manuscript Preparation](https://stm-assoc.org/document/recommendations-for-a-classification-of-ai-use-in-academic-manuscript-preparation/){:target="_blank"} — “This document presents a classification of various ways that AI can be used to assist with preparing academic manuscripts. It may serve as a framework for publishers to develop policies on how AI in manuscript preparation may be used and should be declared by authors.”  
* [Core principles of responsible generative AI usage in research](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s43681-025-00768-8){:target="_blank"} — “This article defines a list of principles guiding the responsible use of GenAI in research, regardless of use case and GenAI technology employed.”  
* [A systematic map of generative AI guidelines and reporting in ecology and evolutionary biology: towards the framework of AI disclosure for Improved Transparency (AIdIT)](https://doi.org/10.1186/s41073-026-00230-1){:target="_blank"}, which systematically maps AI-related journal policies across 230 journals and proposes a standardized, taxonomy-based framework for reporting AI use across all stages of the research lifecycle.

## AI use in peer review {#use-in-peer-review}

The peer review process has also been heavily impacted by the development of AI/LLM tools, with [much discussion surrounding whether and how these tools may hinder or enhance peer review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11858604/){:target="_blank"}. For now, existing policies appear to go in line with manuscript authorship: AI can serve as an auxiliary tool to improve organization, clarity, and grammar of peer review reports, but not as a replacement for any of the critical thinking and expert evaluation steps inherent to peer review. See [Example AI policies from journals & publishers](#example-ai-policies).

One large concern is that the pressure to participate in peer reviews may lead scientists to use these AI tools to replace the more time-intensive parts of the review process, and some evidence suggests this is already the case ([https\://arxiv.org/abs/2403.07183](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.07183)). Anecdotally, editors seem to have indeed noticed an increase in low-quality, superficial peer review reports which may have been generated by AI tools. It has been suggested that open and signed peer reviews might help reduce these issues, although they present their own challenges (See our resources on [Open Peer Review](#open-peer-review)).

Emerging research suggests that scientists increasingly use AI (often without disclosure) to prepare reviews ([Frontiers Media 2025](https://www.frontiersin.org/news/2025/12/15/most-peer-reviewers-now-use-ai-and-publishing-policy-must-keep-pace%20){:target="_blank"}; [Sharma et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1145/3805689.3806505){:target="_blank"}), that in highly controlled environments AI is increasingly capable of producing reviews that are judged by editors as comparable with the best human reviewers ([King and Worthington 2026](https://gking.harvard.edu/peerless/){:target="_blank"}) – but that reviewer use of AI is strongly associated with a reduction in the collective capacity of the review system to discriminate among submission quality tiers ([Sharma et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1145/3805689.3806505){:target="_blank"}). 

## Use in other scientific processes {#use-in-other-processes}

Scientists in many fields are rapidly adopting Generative AI, however the roles filled vary substantially: AI is being used in some areas to generate a wide range of solution candidates for subsequent validation by an established scientific process;  in others to augment or replace established methods for data generation, collection, measurement, and inference; and ubiquitously to supplement the scientific communication process. 

Bourg and colleagues [provide a review of the ways in which Generative AI challenges scientific integrity and reliability](https://doi.org/10.21428/e4baedd9.567bfd15){:target="_blank"}, along with a roadmap for using Generative AI to address these challenges. Michaelov et al. [analyze the role of LLM model transparency in science](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.26539){:target="_blank"} — showing that different categories of use imply different integrity risks and governing responses; and providing guidance for the transparency features most relevant to each use. The European Parliamentary Research Service (EPRS) [argues for the necessity of pro-actively integrating Generative AI into scholarly communication infrastructure](https://www.europarl.europa.eu/thinktank/en/document/EPRS_STU\(2026\)788151){:target="_blank"}, and offers recommendations for support and governance of its use in this area.

## Paper mills & “AI slop” {#ai-slop}

Some editors have reported a recent increase in the number of low-quality submissions at journals, which can impact the editorial team’s ability to process submissions in a timely manner, slowing down the peer review process at journals as well as exhausting editors. Similar issues have been reported with an influx of low-quality preprints being submitted at preprint servers, leading some to increase scrutiny and create stricter rules in an attempt to crack down on these submissions (e.g., [ArXiv will ban researchers for a year if they submit AI-generated content](https://www.404media.co/new-arxiv-rules-ai-generated-papers-ban/){:target="_blank"}). [COPE has a short video about the increase in these AI-generated, poor quality papers](https://publicationethics.org/topic-discussions/artificial-intelligence-ai-and-fake-papers){:target="_blank"} (sometimes termed “AI slop”).

A few suggestions to deal with this increase in low-quality submissions offered by JEDI members include:

* **Streamlined desk-rejection:** create templates for desk-rejecting submissions with very poor fit, that do not sufficiently engage with the discipline’s literature, that are out-of-scope for the journal, etc.  
* **Closing the submission portal** to allow the editorial team enough time to process the existing submissions.  
* [An editorial at Academy of Management Learning & Education](https://journals.aom.org/doi/10.5465/amle.2026.0628){:target="_blank"} reflects the state of AI Journal Policies and makes suggestions for preventing AI slop from overwhelming volunteer editors and reviewers

AI tools which can create scientific-looking papers within seconds have certainly enabled this increase in the volume of low-quality papers, but many have argued there are other important factors to consider as well when dealing with this issue:
